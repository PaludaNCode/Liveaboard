What changed on the last refresh

One entry per refresh: departures added, fares moved, berths sold out. It is a diff between two committed days rather than a claim about today, and it never drops a row without saying so — where a block is capped or a sub-euro move suppressed as source rounding, the report prints the count it left out. A vessel losing every departure is a failed fetch rather than a cancelled season, and is reported as one.

Reading the price columns

They are a bill, left to right: Advertised is the berth price the operator leads with. Nitrox is what breathing gas costs on that boat, or that it is bundled. Mandatory fees is everything on top of the advertised price. Total is what you will actually pay.

A −15% beside the Advertised price means a seller is printing a higher list price next to the one it charges — struck through against every cabin on the booking page, or stated outright by PADI Travel. It is not read from the “20% Off” some operators write into a trip name; that agrees with it everywhere it appears, and misses the sailings discounted without one. Hover it for what the fare is down from and who says so. The On sale filter shows only these. A markdown is what a seller claimed on the day the booking pages were read, and can end overnight.

The Total is the cheapest bill anyone quotes for that sailing, which is not always this site’s own source: where PADI Travel sells the same berth and publishes a complete set of extras, its bill competes for the column and wins when it is lower. A row where the two differ is marked varies; expand it to see both bills under the name of whoever published each.

A row prints one seller’s bill and never a blend of two: when PADI’s wins, the Advertised and Mandatory fees beside it are PADI’s too, so the arithmetic across the row still holds. Expand the row and both bills are there in full, each under the name of whoever published it.

Advertised plus Mandatory fees is the Total, on every row. Sort by Mandatory fees to see which trips hold the most back — but there is no rating of operators here: two boats can reach the same total by pricing very differently, and expanding a row shows how.

Mandatory fees

Marine park and conservation charges, port and harbour dues, fuel surcharges, the visa on arrival, and crew tips wherever an operator publishes a figure. Nothing here is avoidable by declining it, which is why it is in the total rather than beside it.

The two switches above are the exception, and both start on: rental gear and nitrox are in this column until you say otherwise, because that is what most visitors will actually be charged. Turn either off — you own a set, you breathe air — and it leaves the column and the total with it. With both off this column is the unavoidable extras and nothing else.

Why some totals are a range. The range is the operator’s own quote, not our arithmetic: it publishes one fee schedule for the boat, and every trip that boat sells inherits it. Half this fleet does so, and the fees written that way are the park ones — marine park, environment tax, “park and port fees”. Twenty of those operators sell several routes at one trip length, so the route is the only thing the spread can be about: Blue Horizon quotes €90–200 for five different itineraries. Where a boat sells a fortnight as well as a week, the range is often exactly double.

We leave it as a range. Which end of it a particular week lands on depends on where that week goes, and picking a number the operator did not publish would be inventing the very thing this page exists to expose. Expand a row and each fee shows its own range and the operator’s wording for it.

Rental gear

Counted by default, and switching it off changes the order of the table. A full set costs about €200 a week almost regardless of the boat — across 54 vessels the gear price barely tracks the berth price at all.

So it lands hardest on the cheapest trips: 18% on top of a berth in the bottom third, 13% in the top. At the extremes it is 32% of a Hammerhead II week and 9% of a Blue Seas one, which is enough to close most of the gap between them.

Extras with no price

Some operators list an extra and leave the number blank — “Rental Gear”, named and empty. Not zero and not absent: you will be billed, and the listing does not say how much.

Expand the row and each one is named under the bill, so a total built on an incomplete disclosure says so rather than looking finished.

Nitrox and tips

Two thirds of this fleet bundles nitrox and a quarter bills for it: 50 vessels say included, 21 publish a price — €30 to €100, most often around €70 a week — and 6 never mention it at all. Those six read not listed, which is the one thing on this page nitrox can say that is neither yes nor a number: nobody has said whether you will be charged. Four of them are boats whose sailings only PADI Travel lists. That was eleven until this page learned to read the other half of PADI’s disclosure: it states what a fare covers as well as what it charges on top, and the second half had gone unread, so five boats that say included were being reported as silent.

Crew tips are counted wherever an operator publishes a figure; where one is expected but no amount is given the total reads + tips, because that cost is real and sits outside the arithmetic.

Per dive

What a week actually costs you per dive — the comparison most divers are really making, and the reason there is no price per night here. Shown only where the operator publishes a dive count, with that count beside it.

Operators quote a range, and the count shown is the fewest they state — so the price beside it is a ceiling, never a flattering number. The range is real: a week that crosses further, or spends longer inside the marine parks where night dives are not permitted, fits fewer dives into the same seven nights.

Everywhere else it says not stated. It used to assume three dives for every full day at sea, and the ten vessels that do publish a figure show why that had to go: for the same seven-night week they state anything from 15 to 21. At sixty to a hundred euro a dive, six dives is the difference between two boats — which is the entire thing this column is for.

Places

How many berths are left at the advertised price — the total across every room selling at it, since a boat can split them over two. Hover or click the figure for the cabin ladder that price is the bottom of: every rung, what is left on each, and what one person alone pays.

It is the seller’s claim on its own booking page, not a verified count. True when it was read, and stale by morning; the panel says which day, per seller, because the two are read a day apart.

A figure labelled aboard is a different claim and says so: berths left on the sailing at any price, which is what PADI Travel publishes instead of a ladder. It answers “is there room” and not “is there room at this fare”, and the two are never added together or swapped for one another. Where both sellers count, the panel shows both — including the 24 sailings where they disagree about whether the boat is full. Where nobody counted, the column falls back to the operator’s word — available, a few left, sold out — which is the weakest statement of the three and is printed as one.

A 0 means nothing is on sale at the price on the row. Usually the sailing is full; on ten of them it is not, and the cheapest berth you can actually book is 4–25% dearer than the number advertised.

Which boats

Every Egyptian vessel liveaboard.com sells by the berth for these months — not the whole Red Sea fleet. Boats offered only as a whole-boat charter are never linked from the search pages, so nothing here can see them. The count is what a diver can book a single place on, which is the question this page answers.

Disclosure

Whether the operator published its required extras at all. A listing naming only optional ones gets no total: every Egyptian liveaboard pays park and port fees, so silence means either bundled or collected at the dock, and it does not say which.

Sellers

PADI Travel resells many of these berths, and it publishes its own required extras as well as its own price. Both are read here, so what the money columns compare is a total against a total — never a berth price against a bill, which would show whichever seller discloses less as the cheaper one. Where both sell a sailing, the Seller column links each of them, so either price can be checked at the place that published it. On the 230 sailings only PADI lists it links PADI alone and says so, and the Sold by filter above the table is how you narrow to any of the three cases.

They differ more than you would expect for one cabin on one boat on one day: of the trips where both fee books can be added up, 43 of 74 disagree and sixteen by more than €150. One boat’s week is €120 of required extras through one seller and €420 through the other. The berth prices, by contrast, are within €5 nine times in ten — so a comparison of advertised prices alone would have found almost nothing and missed all of this.

Both totals carry the same nitrox and rental gear, from the vessel’s own price list. Those are charged on board, by the boat, to whoever walks up the gangway; they are not the seller’s to set, and leaving them off one side would hand that side about €200 for no reason. What is left is where the two sellers actually differ: the berth, and the fees each one admits to.

Where PADI prices a date but publishes no complete fee book for the trip, there is a second price and no second total, so nothing is claimed: the row keeps this site’s own bill. A sailing PADI does not list at all is evidence of nothing — its calendar runs to a different depth on every boat, and one vessel here lists twenty-two sailings with not one of them in this season.

Entry requirements

The Entry bar column is the certification and the logged dives you need before anyone will take your booking, and the filter above the table has one chip per bar, least demanding first. It is a safety requirement stated by the operator, so it is never softened here and never inferred where nothing was stated — every trip in this season states one, so the column never has to guess.

The two halves are one fact and are filtered as one. Holding Advanced Open Water clears the word Advanced on a trip that also asks for 50 logged dives, and you would still be turned away at the dock; an Open Water week asking for 30 dives is closed to a diver that an Advanced week asking for none would take.

Two sources describe it — the operator’s own itinerary panel and PADI Travel — and both speak about 127 of these trips. They disagree on 60 of those, in both directions: PADI asks for more on 19 and for less on 41. Neither is the authority; both are quoting the same operator. The stricter of the two is shown, marked 2 sellers in the column and named in full when you expand the row, because a diver turned away at the dock has been misled and a diver who over-prepared has not.

Dive sites

Read from the operator’s own description of the trip — the sections it heads with a place, and what its day plan says you dive. Deliberately not from the “key regions” list beside it: that is a summary someone typed once, and on 42 of these trips it names a reef the operator’s own description never mentions. One boat sells a North & Brothers week listing Daedalus, 180 km from anywhere its day plan goes.

The description wins because it is what you would read before booking. What is not read is the body of a description section, which is an essay about a reef and will mention anywhere — one boat introduces Daedalus as “much like the Brothers Islands”, on a week that goes nowhere near them. Individual dives fold into the place you would look for them: the Giannis D is Abu Nuhas, Numidia is the Brothers, Shaab Claudio is Fury Shoal.

Currency

Everything is shown in euro. Amounts an operator quoted in another currency are marked as converted, with the rate and its date, because a conversion is a weaker claim than a quote.
